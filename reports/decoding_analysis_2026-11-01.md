# Decoding-side analysis: where MBR changes MAP's answer, and selective answering

Validation split (7,708 questions), 3 seeds per model, from committed predictions and E3 files only. Generated by `python sandbox/ychai/decoding_analysis.py`; every number is in the JSON next to this file. ± is the sample standard deviation over seeds; brackets are 95% paired bootstrap intervals over questions (10,000 resamples) of each question's mean over seeds. Counts are questions per seed.

MBR is the project's decoder: expected F1 + γ·EM with γ = 1 over the top 10 candidates, grouped by content words (`mbr_utility: normalized`). The *top-1 probability* is the most likely span's probability at the calibrated temperature. The *rival* is the most likely span that doesn't overlap it and has a content word. Both come from E3.

## Findings

1. **MBR changes few answers, and only uncertain ones.** It changes 0.39–1.35% of the answers (row 1: 74 of 7,708). With γ = 1 it cannot change an answer that holds more than half of the candidates' probability (appendix), and in every run every change has top-1 probability below 0.5. On row 1 that leaves 29% of the questions where any MBR with γ ≥ 1 can act.
2. **On row 1, MBR gains by lengthening answers whose boundaries are unclear.** Of its +0.18 [+0.04, +0.31] EM + F1, longer answers (containing MAP's) give +0.24 [+0.12, +0.35]: EM gained on 17.0 questions and lost on 9.0. The gain is on questions torn between boundaries (+0.18 [+0.07, +0.29]); on questions torn between two separate answers it is -0.01 [-0.09, +0.07].
3. **On row 0, MBR hedges between two answers and loses.** Row 0's MBR − MAP is -0.18 [-0.36, -0.02], and the questions torn between two answers alone give -0.12 [-0.20, -0.05]: EM gained on 0.3 and lost on 7.7, and 14 of the 16 changes there are longer answers. E3 points the same way: on the 127 questions where row 0's MBR answers with the span joining the top span and its rival, its EM is 28.9 against MAP's 40.1; on row 1 such answers gain (88 questions, 35.1 → 41.5). The joint head more than halved the cross-span probability (#80), and on row 1 MBR's hedges no longer lose.
4. **The other readers leave MBR little to gain.** The soft target is sure of more answers (only 13% below 0.5), so MBR changes 0.39% of the answers (+0.11 [+0.03, +0.20]). On the risk-loss readers and row 5, longer answers gain only +0.05 to +0.06, losing EM about as often as they gain it. Row 5 also switches to another part of the passage twice as often as row 1 (20.3 against 10.3), losing EM on 7.0 and gaining on 2.3. MBR − MAP: risk share 0.25 +0.05 [-0.09, +0.20], risk share 0.5 +0.03 [-0.09, +0.16], row 5 +0.00 [-0.15, +0.16].
5. **Abstaining moves accuracy far more than decoding.** Answering only its most confident half, row 1 scores 91.1 EM / 95.8 F1, and its most confident 30% 95.1 EM, against 77.0 / 89.5 on everything. MBR adds nothing once abstention starts: ranked by top-1 probability, MAP and MBR give the same answers at every coverage up to 69%, because the questions MBR could change are the first ones abstained on.
6. **Top-1 probability is the better signal for EM; the margin, slightly, for F1.** Top-1 probability has the larger area under the EM curve in all 15 runs (by +0.01 to +0.15), and the margin the larger area under the F1 curve in all 15 (by 0.01 to 0.07). The margin ignores doubt about boundaries, which costs EM but little F1.
7. **Calibrated is not the same as well ranked.** Row 0 ranks its answers better than row 1: area under the EM curve 91.09 ± 0.05 against 90.20 ± 0.04, and 40% of questions answerable at EM ≥ 95 against 30%, though row 1 has the lower ECE (0.012 per group against 0.019 per span). ECE asks whether the probabilities are right on average; selective answering needs the right answers to be the confident ones. The risk-loss readers rank slightly below row 1 and the soft target lowest (15% at EM ≥ 95). Row 0 and row 1 also differ in epochs and windows (plan 9.1), so this doesn't single out the head.

## Appendix: why MBR keeps an answer with more than half the probability

Let g be MAP's answer (the most likely group of boundary variants) with probability p, m ≤ 1 the candidates' total probability, and y any other answer, with probability q ≤ m − p and f = F1(g, y). Different answers have EM 0 with each other, and F1 ≤ 1. Then

- U(g) ≥ (1 + γ)·p + f·q (g against itself and against y);
- U(y) ≤ (1 + γ)·q + f·p + (m − p − q) (y against itself, against g, and at most 1 against the rest).

So U(g) − U(y) ≥ (2 + γ − f)·p − (γ − f)·q − m. For γ ≥ 1 ≥ f the q term is never positive, and q ≤ m − p gives U(g) − U(y) ≥ (1 + γ − f)·(2p − m), which is positive once p > m / 2: MBR returns g. A top-1 span with probability ≥ 0.5 belongs to a group with at least that much, which no other group can beat, so that group is g and p ≥ 0.5 ≥ m / 2.

With γ < 1 the guarantee weakens to p > m / (1 + γ), and with γ = 0 (expected F1 alone) it is gone. For example, "A B" with probability 0.55 and "B C" with 0.45 give the candidate "A B C", even with almost no probability of its own, an expected F1 of 0.80 against 0.775 for "A B". With γ = 1, "A B" keeps the lead (1.325 against 0.80).

A random check through `squadqa.decode` agrees (`half_rule_check`, seed 0). Of 20,000 random candidate sets, 8,878 have MAP's answer above half the candidates' probability. MBR changes that answer in 0 of them with γ = 1, 0 with γ = 2 and 0 with γ = 0.5, against 228 with γ = 0.
